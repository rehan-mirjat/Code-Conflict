What is Social Engineering?

The art of exploiting human psychology rather than technical hacking techniques to gain access to systems or data.

Why it works:
  • Exploits natural human tendencies to trust and help
  • Bypasses even the strongest technical security controls
  • Often more effective than technical attacks
  • Low cost for attackers with high potential rewards

Psychological Principles Exploited

Authority

People tend to obey authority figures, even when asked to do something questionable.

Example: "This is IT, we need your password"

Likability

We're more likely to comply with requests from people we like or find attractive.

Example: Flattery before requests

Urgency

Creating time pressure short-circuits rational decision making.

Example: "Your account will be closed in 1 hour!"

Reciprocity

People feel obligated to return favors, even unsolicited ones.

Example: Free gift before asking for info

Social Proof

We follow the crowd, assuming others' actions are correct.

Example: "All your coworkers already did this"

Scarcity

Limited availability increases perceived value and urgency.

Example: "Only 3 spots left for this offer"

Common Social Engineering Techniques

Phishing Most Common

Fraudulent attempts to obtain sensitive information by disguising as trustworthy entity.

  • Channel: Email, SMS, social media
  • Goal: Credentials, financial info
  • Example: "Your account has been compromised" email
Pretexting Highly Effective

Creating a fabricated scenario to engage a target and extract information.

  • Channel: Phone, in-person
  • Goal: Sensitive data, access
  • Example: "I'm from HR, need to verify your details"
Baiting Physical

Offering something enticing to lure victims into a trap.

  • Channel: Physical media, downloads
  • Goal: Malware installation
  • Example: "Free software" on USB drives in parking lot
Quid Pro Quo Exchange

Offering a service or benefit in exchange for information or access.

  • Channel: Phone, email
  • Goal: Credentials, access
  • Example: "IT support" offering free help for login

Business Email Compromise (BEC) "CEO Fraud" attacks targeting organizations

How BEC Works

Attack Flow:
  1. Research targets (LinkedIn, company website)
  2. Spoof executive's email or compromise account
  3. Send urgent request to finance/HR
  4. Request wire transfer or sensitive data
  5. Funds transferred to attacker's account
Red Flags:
  • Urgent requests bypassing normal procedures
  • Slight email variations (CEO@company-inc.com)
  • Requests for secrecy ("Just between us")
  • Unusual payment instructions
  • Last-minute changes to payment details
Protection & Defense-in-Depth:

Implement mandatory multi-party authorization for financial wire transfers and establish out-of-band callback verification using known, verified numbers.

Emerging Threat: AI Voice Cloning & Deepfake BEC

Adversaries now synthesize executive voices from podcast and earnings call audio to call finance teams with urgent verbal transfer orders (demonstrated in major enterprise fraud cases). Defend against synthetic media via predetermined verbal duress challenge phrases and strict cryptographic transaction signing.

Social Engineering Protection

Organizational Defenses

  1. Regular security awareness training
  2. Clear verification procedures for sensitive requests
  3. Multi-factor authentication for all accounts
  4. Simulated phishing exercises
  5. Reporting system for suspicious contacts

Individual Best Practices

  • Verify unexpected requests through known channels
  • Don't trust caller ID or email display names
  • Be wary of urgency or secrecy demands
  • Never plug in found USB drives
  • Question requests that bypass normal procedures

Pretexting Attack Simulation & Case Study
Case Study: Scattered Spider IT Help Desk Pretext (MGM Resorts)

Adversaries gathered employee names and job titles from LinkedIn. The attacker phoned the internal corporate IT helpdesk, posing as that employee claiming they were locked out of their smartphone, and requested an MFA reset/new Okta token. The helpdesk technician complied without biometric or in-person manager validation, giving the cartel direct SSO access that shut down casino operations.

Defensive Mitigations:
  • Enforce out-of-band manager video verification before resetting authenticator tokens or issuing new temporary access passes.
  • Deploy FIDO2/WebAuthn hardware tokens (YubiKeys) which are technically resilient against MFA helpdesk resets and reverse proxy relays.
  • Flag helpdesk calls originating from spoofed or non-corporate registered phone lines using caller ID verification (STIR/SHAKEN).
Baiting Attack Vectors & Physical Security
Physical "Rubber Ducky" Drop

Attackers drop USB drives labeled "Q4 Executive Salaries.xlsx" or "Company Layoffs Masterlist" in parking lots or lobbies. Curtailed users plug the drive in out of curiosity.

Quid Pro Quo Scenarios & Social Engineering Defenses
Scenario: The "Helpful" Vendor Support Call

An attacker calls random internal extensions pretending to be from internal network support: "We are pushing a mandatory VPN security patch that fixes connectivity hiccups. If you let me connect via AnyDesk/Quick Assist and enter your domain credentials, I will finish your update in 2 minutes so your workstation won't freeze." Victims perceive the offer as a helpful time-saver and willingly bypass access protocols.

Verification Protocol:

Legitimate IT departments never ask users for passwords, nor do they request unannounced remote desktop sessions without an existing support ticket number.

  • Check the IT service management (ITSM) ticketing portal for an open ticket before consenting to any remote support session.
  • Block unauthorized remote management tools (AnyDesk, TeamViewer, RustDesk) at network perimeter firewalls and endpoint AppLocker rules.
